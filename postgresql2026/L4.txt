LEVEL 4 OVERVIEW
-----------------
At L1 you identified. At L2 you understood. At L3 you operated.
At L4 you DESIGN and LEAD.

You are no longer executing tasks handed to you — you are designing the
systems, processes, and architectures that others will operate. You make
technology choices, justify them commercially, mentor junior DBAs, and own
the strategic direction of the database environment.

L4 tasks are longer, ambiguous, and cross-functional. There are often no
single right answers. You must reason from first principles, defend your
decisions, and adapt when constraints change. You will write runbooks,
design standards, propose architectures, and lead from the front.

L4 Promotion Criteria: You will be ready for L5 when your outputs show
original thinking, defensible architectural decisions, team-level impact,
and commercial awareness. Correct execution alone is not enough — you must
also be able to teach what you know and challenge what you inherit.

TASK TITLE: Design a Multi-Region Active-Passive Database Architecture
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
A single-region database is a single point of failure for the entire business.
Designing multi-region means surviving data center loss. At L4 you do not
just configure replication — you architect the topology, choose consistency
guarantees, design the failover automation, and cost it out.

SCENARIO
The CTO asks: "Design a database architecture that can survive the complete
loss of our primary AWS region (eu-west-1). We have users in Europe and Asia.
The business cannot tolerate more than 60 seconds of downtime and 30 seconds
of data loss."

REQUIREMENTS
- RTO: 60 seconds
- RPO: 30 seconds
- Regions: eu-west-1 (primary), ap-southeast-1 (DR)
- Traffic: 80% EU, 20% Asia
- Database: PostgreSQL 16, 500GB, 2000 TPS at peak

YOUR TASK (Architect, document, cost, defend)
1. Choose replication mode:
   - Synchronous replication: RPO = 0, but adds latency to every write
     (EU→Asia round trip = ~170ms; your write latency becomes 170ms+)
   - Asynchronous replication: RPO = lag (could be 30 sec), writes stay fast
   - Semi-synchronous (synchronous_commit=remote_apply): hybrid, most practical
   Justify your choice for a 30-second RPO vs synchronous cost.

2. Design the topology:
   - Primary in eu-west-1 with synchronous_commit = remote_write to ap-southeast-1
   - ap-southeast-1 standby with hot standby enabled (can serve reads)
   - WAL archiving to S3 (both regions) for PITR beyond the standby
   - Read replicas in each region for reporting workloads

3. Design automatic failover:
   - Failure detection: if primary is unreachable for 15 seconds, failover begins
   - Tool choice: pg_auto_failover vs Patroni vs custom script — justify
   - Automation: promote ap-southeast-1 standby, update DNS (Route 53 or similar)
   - Total time: detection (15s) + promote (5s) + DNS TTL (30s) = ~50s — meets RTO

4. Design the connection layer:
   - Application connects to a virtual IP or DNS-based endpoint
   - On failover, DNS updates, applications reconnect automatically
   - Connection pooler (PgBouncer) in each region shields apps from failover
     reconnection storms

5. Design the data flow:
   - Writes: eu-west-1 primary, replicated async to ap-southeast-1
   - EU reads: local replica in eu-west-1
   - Asia reads: ap-southeast-1 standby (may be slightly stale — is this acceptable?)

6. Cost the architecture:
   - 2× EC2 instances for primary + standby
   - 2× read replicas (one per region)
   - S3 storage for WAL archive (ongoing)
   - Data transfer costs (WAL streaming cross-region)
   - PgBouncer instances × 2 regions
   Produce a rough monthly cost estimate.

7. Write the architecture decision record (ADR):
   - Context: What problem are we solving?
   - Decision: What architecture did we choose?
   - Consequences: What do we gain? What do we give up?
   - Alternatives considered: What did we reject and why?

SAFETY
Risk: This is a design task — no system changes. The output is a document.

SKILL BUILT
Area  : Database Server / Instance
Skill : Multi-region architecture design — topology, failover automation, cost

REVIEW QUESTION

partition between eu-west-1 and ap-southeast-1 lasts 90 seconds before the
primary fails. During that 90 seconds, 3,000 transactions committed on the
primary but never reached the standby. You failover. How does the application
discover which transactions were lost? Is this 30-second-RPO or 90-second-RPO?

TASK TITLE: Build a PostgreSQL Runbook for On-Call Engineers
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
At L3 you handled incidents. At L4 you make it possible for others to handle
them without you. A runbook translates your expertise into a structured,
followable guide for someone who may not be a DBA but is on-call at 3 AM.
A good runbook shortens MTTR. A bad one makes things worse.

SCENARIO
Your team is growing. Junior engineers and SREs are joining the on-call
rotation. They are not PostgreSQL experts. You must produce a runbook
that enables them to handle the top 8 most common PostgreSQL incidents
competently.

YOUR TASK (Design and write the runbook)
1. Identify the 8 most common incidents in your environment:
   Based on your L3 work, you know these happen regularly:
   - Connection pool exhaustion
   - High replication lag (>5 minutes)
   - Backup failure (no backup in 25+ hours)
   - Disk usage >85%
   - Long-running query blocking others (lock wait)
   - Autovacuum not keeping up (dead tuples >30%)
   - Primary PostgreSQL process not responding
   - Slow queries (P95 latency doubled)

2. For each incident, write a structured runbook section:

   SECTION FORMAT:
   ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   INCIDENT: [Name]
   SEVERITY: [P1 / P2 / P3]
   SYMPTOM: What the alert says or what the user reports
   DETECTION: How to confirm this is actually happening
   IMPACT: What breaks if you do nothing
   IMMEDIATE ACTION: What to do in the first 5 minutes
   INVESTIGATION: Step-by-step diagnostic commands
   RESOLUTION: What to do, in order, with commands
   ESCALATION: When to wake the senior DBA
   ROLLBACK: How to undo your action if it makes things worse
   POST-INCIDENT: What to do after it is resolved
   ────────────────────────────────────────────────────

3. Write the runbook for all 8 incidents using that format.

4. Make it safe for non-DBAs:
   - Every command must be copy-paste ready (no "run the appropriate query")
   - Every destructive action must be preceded by a WARNING block
   - Assume the reader has never used psql before (link to basics)

5. Add a quick reference decision tree at the front:
   "Users say DB is slow → check connections first (see Incident #1)
    → if connections are fine, check locks (see Incident #5)
    → if no locks, check slow queries (see Incident #8)"

6. Review the runbook:
   - Give it to a non-DBA colleague. Can they follow it?
   - What was confusing? Rewrite it.
   - Test: simulate an incident and time how long the runbook takes to follow.

SAFETY
Risk: Medium
A runbook that gives bad commands to on-call engineers during an incident
can cause more damage. Every command must be verified and safe. All
destructive commands must have a warning.

SKILL BUILT
Area  : Database Server / Instance
Skill : Runbook design and authoring — incident documentation for non-experts

TASK TITLE: Evaluate PostgreSQL vs Alternatives and Produce a Technology Selection Report
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
A senior DBA is expected to have opinions about when PostgreSQL is the right
choice and when it is not. Technology selection reports prevent expensive
decisions made on hype rather than evidence. A DBA at L4 can write one.

SCENARIO
Your product team asks: "We're designing a new system to process 50 million
IoT sensor readings per day with complex time-series queries. Should we use
PostgreSQL, TimescaleDB, InfluxDB, or ClickHouse? Give us a recommendation."

YOUR TASK (Technology evaluation)
1. Understand the requirements:
   - Write volume: 50M rows/day ≈ 580 inserts/second sustained
   - Query patterns: aggregate by time window, sensor ID, rolling averages
   - Data retention: keep 2 years hot, archive older
   - Team expertise: team knows SQL, not NoSQL
   - Integration: existing PostgreSQL infrastructure

2. Evaluate each option against requirements:

   PostgreSQL (native):
   - Strengths: team familiarity, ACID, rich SQL, existing infrastructure
   - Weaknesses: time-series queries are not native, partition management manual
   - Performance at 580 inserts/sec: feasible on good hardware
   - Verdict: workable but not optimised

   TimescaleDB (PostgreSQL extension):
   - Strengths: SQL-compatible, native time-series functions, automatic partitioning
   - Weaknesses: commercial tier for clustering, adds complexity
   - Performance: purpose-built for this workload, 10-100x faster than raw PG
   - Verdict: best fit if team stays in PostgreSQL ecosystem

   InfluxDB:
   - Strengths: best-in-class time-series performance, purpose-built
   - Weaknesses: different query language (InfluxQL/Flux), no SQL joins
   - Performance: highest write throughput of all candidates
   - Verdict: best raw performance, worst ecosystem fit

   ClickHouse:
   - Strengths: columnar storage, excellent for OLAP aggregations
   - Weaknesses: not designed for high-frequency inserts, eventual consistency
   - Performance: fastest for read aggregations, but ingestion needs batching
   - Verdict: better for analytics after ingestion, not the write path

3. Apply a scoring matrix:
   Define criteria weights (write throughput, query speed, operational complexity,
   team expertise, cost, ecosystem fit) and score each option 1-5.
   Show your working.

4. Produce the recommendation:
   Recommend TimescaleDB as the optimal choice, with rationale. Acknowledge
   what is sacrificed. Define the conditions under which you would revisit.

5. Write the report (2-4 pages):
   - Executive summary (1 paragraph)
   - Requirements analysis
   - Candidate evaluation
   - Scoring matrix
   - Recommendation + rationale
   - Risks and mitigations
   - Decision owner and review date

SAFETY
Risk: None (design and research task)

SKILL BUILT
Area  : Database Server / Instance
Skill : Technology evaluation — structured analysis, scoring matrices, commercial reports

REVIEW QUESTION

scored higher on performance. Why didn't you choose it?" Write a 3-paragraph
defence of your recommendation that addresses performance while explaining
why it is not the only factor.

TASK TITLE: Mentor a Junior DBA Through Their First Production Incident
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Technical expertise without the ability to transfer it does not scale.
At L4 you multiply your impact by developing others. Mentoring during a
live incident is the most intense and effective form of knowledge transfer.

SCENARIO
A junior DBA (6 months of experience, L1/L2 level) is on-call when a
production alert fires: "Autovacuum not running, dead tuple count critical."
They call you. Instead of taking over, you guide them through it.

YOUR TASK (Mentoring framework)
1. Design your mentoring approach before the incident:
   - Will you take over the keyboard or coach from the side?
   - What is the junior DBA's actual knowledge level? (Establish this first)
   - What do they know about VACUUM? (Ask, don't assume)

2. Guide the incident (not fix it yourself):
   - Ask them to explain what the alert means (what is a dead tuple?)
   - Ask them what commands they would run to investigate
   - Fill in gaps: "Have you looked at pg_stat_user_tables yet?"
   - Let them run the commands and interpret the output
   - Redirect if they go in the wrong direction

3. Key teaching moments to hit during this incident:
   - Why autovacuum exists and what happens without it
   - The difference between autovacuum not running vs not keeping up
   - How to check if a table's autovacuum is blocked (long-running transactions?)
   - How to manually trigger vacuum safely
   - When to escalate vs when to resolve independently

4. Post-incident debrief (the most important part):
   - Sit with them for 30 minutes after
   - Ask: "What did you learn?"
   - Correct misconceptions gently
   - Ask: "What would you do differently next time?"
   - Assign them to write up the incident and solution (their words, not yours)

5. Follow-up:
   - One week later: simulate the same incident again, without your help
   - Did they remember? What still needed reinforcement?
   - Document their progress (for performance reviews and your own coaching)

6. Build a mentoring plan for this DBA:
   - What are their current gaps? (Identify top 5)
   - What incidents or tasks would build those skills?
   - What is the timeline to get them to L3 independence?

SAFETY
Risk: Medium
Letting a junior DBA make decisions on production requires careful oversight.
Be ready to intervene if they are about to cause damage. Coach from the side,
but keep your hand near the brake.

Area  : Database Server / Instance
Skill : Mentoring and knowledge transfer — incident-based coaching

TASK TITLE: Design a Database Platform Engineering Strategy
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
A DBA team that manually provisions every database is a bottleneck. Platform
engineering moves the DBA from ticket-taker to platform builder — you build
the systems that enable teams to self-serve safely and consistently.

SCENARIO
Your organisation has 12 engineering teams, 80+ databases, 3 DBAs. The DBA
team is overwhelmed with provisioning requests, access control changes, and
minor incident tickets. You need a platform strategy to scale the DBA impact
without scaling the headcount.

YOUR TASK (Platform strategy design)
1. Categorise current DBA work by effort and repetition:
   - High effort + repetitive: provisioning (target for automation)
   - High effort + unique: incident response, architecture (keep manual)
   - Low effort + repetitive: permission grants, size queries (self-service)
   - Low effort + unique: ad-hoc queries (minimal investment)

2. Design the platform components:

   Component A: Self-service provisioning API
   - Engineers submit a provisioning request (name, owner, size, purpose)
   - API validates against standards (naming, disk, limits)
   - API provisions database, roles, schemas, backups, monitoring automatically
   - DBA is notified; reviews only edge cases

   Component B: Standardised database templates
   - OLTP template: optimised for high-concurrency transactional workloads
   - Analytics template: optimised for batch read/aggregation
   - Development template: minimal resources, auto-expired in 30 days
   Engineers choose a template; platform applies known-good settings

   Component C: Infrastructure-as-code (Terraform / Ansible)
   - All database infrastructure defined in code
   - Changes reviewed via pull request (DBA is code reviewer, not executor)
   - Reproducible: deploy the same database config to dev/staging/prod

   Component D: Centralised monitoring
   - All databases send metrics to central platform
   - Dashboards visible to all teams
   - Alerts routed to the owning team, not just DBAs

3. Define the DBA role in the new model:
   - Platform builder: build and maintain the provisioning API
   - Code reviewer: review infrastructure changes (not executor)
   - Incident specialist: handle incidents that self-service cannot resolve
   - Advisor: advise teams on schema design, query optimisation

4. Design the transition plan:
   - Phase 1 (Month 1-3): Build provisioning API
   - Phase 2 (Month 4-6): Migrate existing databases to IaC
   - Phase 3 (Month 7-9): Train teams on self-service
   - Phase 4 (Month 10+): DBA team focuses on platform improvements

5. Measure success:
   - Metric 1: Provisioning request turnaround (from 3 days to 15 minutes)
   - Metric 2: DBA ticket volume (reduce by 60% in 6 months)
   - Metric 3: Database incidents (reduce by 40% — better standards prevent them)

SAFETY
Risk: Medium
Platform automation amplifies mistakes as well as successes. Robust validation,
testing, and audit trails are mandatory.

Area  : Database Server / Instance

TASK TITLE: Lead a Post-Incident Review for a Major Outage
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
A major outage leaves technical debt: broken trust, unresolved root causes,
demoralised team. The post-incident review is your opportunity to transform
a failure into systemic improvement. At L4 you lead this process.

SCENARIO
Three days ago, a 4-hour outage occurred. Root cause: a developer ran
ALTER TABLE on a heavily trafficked table without a maintenance window.
PostgreSQL held an access exclusive lock, blocking all reads and writes.
Connection pool filled. Applications failed. $200,000 of revenue lost.
Five engineering teams are upset. The CTO wants answers.

YOUR TASK (Lead the postmortem)
1. Prepare the review meeting:
   - Invite: DBA team, DevOps, Engineering leads, CTO (optional)
   - Set tone: blameless. The goal is systemic improvement, not punishment.
   - Gather: timeline of events, all relevant logs, all actions taken

2. Facilitate the review (design the agenda):
   - 5 min: Review the timeline (factual, no opinions)
   - 15 min: Five Whys to root cause (what could have prevented this?)
   - 20 min: Identify contributing factors (what made this worse?)
   - 20 min: Design action items (what changes prevent recurrence?)
   - 10 min: Assign owners and deadlines

3. Guide the root cause analysis:
   The immediate cause was the ALTER TABLE. But the deeper causes were:
   - No mechanism preventing schema changes during business hours
   - No monitoring that detected the growing lock before it was catastrophic
   - No procedure requiring DBA approval for DDL on live tables
   - No rehearsal or canary process for DDL changes

4. Design the corrective actions:
   - Short-term (this week): document DDL change procedure
   - Medium-term (1 month): implement DDL review approval gate
   - Long-term (3 months): build monitoring for lock wait chains

5. Write the postmortem document:
   - Summary (3 sentences): what happened, impact, resolution
   - Timeline (factual, timestamped)
   - Root cause analysis (5 Whys tree)
   - Contributing factors
   - Action items (owner, deadline, success metric)
   - Lessons learned (what worked in the response, what did not)

6. Present to the CTO:
   - Do not speculate about blame
   - Do not over-promise ("this will never happen again")
   - Present: what happened, what we learned, what we are doing about it
   - Acknowledge the impact without defensiveness

SAFETY
Risk: Medium (organisational/people risk)
Blameless postmortems require facilitation skill. Avoid letting the meeting
become a blame session. If it heads that way, redirect explicitly.

SKILL BUILT
Area  : Database Server / Instance
Skill : Postmortem leadership — blameless analysis, action item design, executive presentation

REVIEW QUESTION
The CTO asks after the meeting: "Who is responsible for this?" How do you

TASK TITLE: Architect a Zero-Downtime Major Version Upgrade Strategy
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
pg_upgrade requires downtime. For large databases, even 30 minutes is
unacceptable. At L4 you design a zero-downtime upgrade strategy using
logical replication, shadow instances, and application-level versioning.

SCENARIO
You must upgrade 6 production databases (total 2TB) from PostgreSQL 14
to PostgreSQL 16. Business requires: zero planned downtime (applications
run 24/7 globally). You have a 5-minute maintenance window maximum.

YOUR TASK (Design zero-downtime upgrade)
1. Understand why pg_upgrade cannot be zero-downtime:
   - pg_upgrade stops the old cluster, migrates, starts the new
   - For 2TB at 500MB/s: 4,000 seconds (67 minutes) minimum
   - This is categorically too long. You need a different approach.

2. Design the logical replication approach:
   Step 1: Build a new PostgreSQL 16 instance (empty)
   Step 2: On PG14 primary, create a publication for all tables
   Step 3: On PG16 instance, create a subscription to the PG14 publication
   Step 4: Let replication sync (may take hours for 2TB)
   Step 5: When lag is near zero (< 1 second), execute the cutover:
           - Stop writes to PG14 (set to read-only or pause app)
           - Wait for final WAL to apply on PG16 (seconds)
           - Redirect application to PG16
           - Resume writes
   Total write-pause duration: 5–30 seconds

3. Identify limitations of logical replication upgrade:
   - Schema must be manually migrated first (DDL is not replicated)
   - Sequences must be advanced on PG16 to avoid collisions
   - Large objects (pg_largeobject) are NOT replicated
   - Unlogged tables are NOT replicated
   - Extension-specific objects may not replicate cleanly

4. Design the migration runbook for 6 databases:
   - Sequence: upgrade in order of criticality (least critical first)
   - Lessons from each inform the next
   - Build rollback step at each stage

5. Design the application cutover mechanism:
   - Application reads a config flag: "DB_HOST = pg14 or pg16"
   - At cutover moment: flip the flag (< 1 second)
   - New connections go to PG16; in-flight on PG14 complete then stop
   - Connection pooler handles reconnection transparently

6. Write the zero-downtime upgrade runbook including:
   - Pre-upgrade checklist (6 items minimum)
   - Execution steps with timing estimates
   - Validation steps after each stage
   - Rollback procedure at each stage
   - Post-upgrade cleanup

SAFETY
Risk: HIGH
Logical replication upgrade is complex and can fail silently (missing data,
schema drift). Test the entire procedure multiple times on copies before
executing on production.

YOUR TASK (Write the standards document)

   NAMING STANDARDS
   - Databases: {service}_{env} e.g. billing_prod, auth_staging
   - Roles: {purpose}_{db}_{access} e.g. app_billing_rw, dba_admin

   - Off-site copy mandatory (different region)

   - shared_buffers: 25% of available RAM
   - All production indexes created CONCURRENTLY
   - Query P95 latency target documented per database
   - pg_stat_statements mandatory on all production instances

   - All schema changes via pull request with DBA review
   - No DDL on production without approved change ticket
   - All changes must have rollback procedure documented
   - Changes executed only in approved maintenance windows

   - The rule (precise, testable)
   - The rationale (why this matters)
   - The exception process (how to get a waiver)

   - Who can propose changes? (PR process)

TASK TITLE: Design a Logical Replication Architecture for Decoupled Read Scaling
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Physical streaming replication scales reads but ties the standby to the
primary's exact schema. Logical replication allows you to replicate a
subset of tables to specialised databases (analytics, reporting) with
different schemas or versions.

SCENARIO
Your OLTP database is overwhelmed by reporting queries. The product team
runs heavy analytical queries that take 30 seconds, blocking OLTP traffic.
You must architect a read-scaling solution that decouples analytics from OLTP.

YOUR TASK (Logical replication architecture)
1. Evaluate decoupling options:
   - Physical standby (streaming replication): exact copy, reporting queries
     still run the same heavy queries — does not help with query performance
   - Logical replication to analytics replica: can have different indexes,
     different schema, different PostgreSQL version
   - ETL pipeline to data warehouse: most decoupled, highest latency
   - Materialised views refreshed from replica: good for known queries

2. Design the logical replication topology:
   - Source: OLTP primary (PostgreSQL 16)
   - Publication: all tables used in reporting (not everything)
   - Subscription: analytics PostgreSQL instance
   - Analytics replica: different indexes (columnar-friendly), aggregation tables

3. Design the analytics schema:
   - Denormalise for query performance (avoid joins)
   - Add pre-aggregated summary tables (refresh via trigger or cron)
   - Add indexes appropriate for analytical queries (not for OLTP inserts)
   - Different retention (keep 5 years on analytics, 1 year on OLTP)

4. Manage replication lag:
   - Acceptable lag for analytics: 5 minutes (not real-time)
   - Alert if lag > 30 minutes
   - Application must know: "analytics data may be 5 minutes old"

5. Design the failure mode:
   - Analytics replica fails: OLTP continues unaffected (logical replication is async)
   - OLTP primary fails: analytics goes read-only but still works
   - Replication slot fills up (lag too large): WAL builds on primary — monitor

6. Document application routing:
   - OLTP queries (writes, real-time reads): primary connection string
   - Analytics queries (reports, dashboards): analytics replica connection string
   - Applications must be coded to use the correct endpoint

SAFETY
Risk: Medium
Logical replication slots retain WAL on the primary until consumed. If the
analytics replica falls too far behind, the primary disk fills up.

SCENARIO

   - Credentials are valid for 1 hour and auto-expire

   - Phase 5: Enable automatic rotation

   - No credentials in git (enforced by pre-commit hook)
   - All service account credentials managed by Vault

TASK TITLE: Design a Blue-Green Database Deployment Strategy
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Blue-green deployment is a zero-downtime release strategy. At L4 you design
this for databases — which is harder than for stateless applications because
databases carry state that must be consistent across both environments.

SCENARIO
Your team wants to deploy major schema changes with zero downtime. Traditional
deployments require maintenance windows. Blue-green would allow instant
rollback if the new schema breaks something.

YOUR TASK (Blue-green database design)
1. Understand the challenge:
   - Blue environment: current production (schema v1)
   - Green environment: new production (schema v2)
   - Traffic cutover: switch from blue to green (immediate)
   - Rollback: switch back from green to blue (within 5 minutes)
   - Problem: writes to green during cutover cannot roll back to blue

2. Design for backward-compatible schema changes:
   - Rule: every migration must be backward-compatible
   - New column: add as nullable first (old code ignores it, new code uses it)
   - Renamed column: add new column, copy data, keep old column, drop old later
   - New table: safe to add at any time
   - Dropped table: never drop until old code is gone

3. Design the traffic switching mechanism:
   - Connection string config: blue_endpoint vs green_endpoint
   - PgBouncer as a proxy: switch target without application restart
   - DNS-based: update DNS TTL to redirect traffic in seconds

4. Design write reconciliation for rollback:
   - Scenario: traffic on green for 10 minutes, then rollback to blue
   - Writes that happened on green: must be replicated to blue before rollback
   - Use logical replication from green to blue (bidirectional)
   - On rollback: flip replication direction, wait for sync, switch

5. Define which changes CANNOT be blue-green:
   - Non-backward-compatible changes (column type change) require a maintenance window
   - When: document this clearly in the migration policy

6. Write the blue-green deployment procedure (step-by-step):
   - Prerequisites (what must be true before starting)
   - Deploy to green (schema + application)
   - Validate green (smoke tests, health checks)
   - Traffic cutover (how, timing, communications)
   - Monitoring (what to watch for 15 minutes post-cutover)
   - Rollback trigger (what conditions trigger rollback?)
   - Rollback execution (steps, timing, communications)

SAFETY
Risk: HIGH — bidirectional replication is complex and can cause conflicts.
Test extensively. Have a clear abort criterion.

TASK TITLE: Design a Zero-Trust Database Access Model
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Traditional network-perimeter security assumes internal users are trusted.
Zero-trust assumes no one is trusted by default — every access request is
verified explicitly. At L4 you design this for databases.

SCENARIO
Your organisation is moving to a zero-trust security model. The security
team asks: "How do we implement zero-trust for database access?" You must
design the answer.

YOUR TASK (Zero-trust database architecture)
1. Define zero-trust principles as applied to databases:
   - Never trust network location (being inside the VPC is not enough)
   - Verify identity explicitly (every connection authenticated, not just first)
   - Least privilege access (rights scoped to minimum necessary, time-limited)
   - Assume breach (monitor all access as if an attacker is present)

2. Design the identity layer:
   - All database connections identified by: individual identity (not just IP)
   - Service accounts: unique per service, not shared
   - Human access: via identity provider (SSO) + Vault ephemeral credentials
   - Certificate-based: each service has a unique client certificate

   - Before a connection is allowed: identity verified, role checked, time checked
   - Access policies: "app_billing can connect to billing_prod during business hours"
   - Policy violations: logged, alerted, blocked
   - Policy review: quarterly access certification (each owner confirms still needed)

4. Design continuous verification:
   - Sessions re-authenticated every hour (not just at connect time)
   - Anomalous queries trigger step-up authentication
   - Session terminated if behaviour deviates from baseline

5. Design the audit layer:
   - Every query logged (pg_audit extension or custom trigger)
   - Logs immutable (sent to external SIEM immediately)
   - Anomaly detection: query volume, data volume, unusual patterns

6. Write the zero-trust database policy:
   - What is required? (certificate + identity + Vault credentials)
   - What is monitored? (all access, all queries)
   - What triggers an alert? (anomalous volumes, access outside hours)
   - What triggers a forced disconnect? (confirmed breach indicators)

TASK TITLE: Design and Implement Column-Level Encryption for PII
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Database-level encryption protects data at rest, but if someone gains access
to the database, they can read all data. Column-level encryption protects
specific fields (SSN, medical records) even from privileged database users.

SCENARIO
A GDPR audit requires that all personally identifiable information (PII) be
encrypted such that a database superuser cannot read the raw values without
the encryption key. Design and implement column-level encryption.

YOUR TASK (Column-level encryption design)
1. Identify which columns require column-level encryption:
   - customers.email → PII (TIER 3)
   - customers.phone → PII (TIER 3)
   - patients.national_id → TIER 4
   - employees.salary → CONFIDENTIAL (TIER 3)

2. Choose the encryption approach:
   - pgcrypto extension: AES encryption within PostgreSQL (key must be passed per query)
   - Application-level encryption: application encrypts before INSERT (most secure)
   - Transparent Data Encryption (TDE): coming in PostgreSQL 17 (future option)
   Recommend: application-level for highest security, pgcrypto for simplicity.

3. Implement with pgcrypto:
   - Store encrypted values: UPDATE customers SET email =
       pgp_sym_encrypt(email, 'encryption_key') WHERE ...;
   - Read decrypted values: SELECT pgp_sym_decrypt(email::bytea, 'encryption_key')
       FROM customers;
   - The key is passed at query time — a superuser who can read queries sees the key

4. Implement application-level (more secure):
   - Application encrypts email before sending to PostgreSQL
   - PostgreSQL stores the ciphertext (cannot decrypt without the key)
   - Even a superuser running SELECT * sees only ciphertext

5. Design key management:
   - Encryption keys stored in Vault (not in the database)
   - Key rotation: encrypt all existing rows with new key, update key version
   - Emergency access: if Vault is unavailable, encrypted data is inaccessible

6. Write the encryption policy:
   - Which columns are encrypted?
   - Where are keys stored?
   - Who can access keys?
   - What is the rotation schedule?
   - How is compliance verified (spot check decryption)?

SAFETY
Risk: HIGH — if you lose the encryption key, you lose the data permanently.
Key management is as important as the encryption itself.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Point-in-Time Recovery is not just "restore from backup." In a DR context it
is an architecture: where is WAL stored, how is it fetched during recovery,
how is recovery orchestrated, how is the recovery target determined? You
design all of this.

SCENARIO
You are designing the DR architecture for a financial services database.
Requirements:
- RPO: 0 (no data loss) or 30 seconds maximum
- RTO: 5 minutes from declared disaster to first queries accepted
- Retention: WAL must be available for 7 years (regulatory)
- Geography: primary UK, DR USA (separate legal jurisdiction)

YOUR TASK (PITR-centric DR design)
1. Design the WAL pipeline:
   - Primary writes WAL → archive_command ships to S3 (UK) every 1 segment
   - S3 (UK) replicates to S3 (USA) via S3 Cross-Region Replication (CRR)
   - DR PostgreSQL instance has restore_command that fetches from S3 (USA)
   - This gives both PITR capability and geographic redundancy

2. Choose RPO approach:
   - RPO=0: synchronous replication to DR (adds ~100ms write latency trans-Atlantic)
   - RPO=30s: asynchronous streaming replication to DR standby
   - RPO=30s using WAL archive: no streaming, but WAL segments every 30 seconds
   Select RPO=30s streaming replication (acceptable latency, acceptable RPO).

3. Design the recovery automation:
   - Failure detection: 15 seconds (monitoring heartbeat)
   - Decision gate: automated (if primary unreachable for 15s) or manual (if ambiguous)
   - Promotion: pg_promote() on DR standby (< 5 seconds)
   - DNS failover: Route 53 health check triggers automatic DNS update (< 60 seconds)
   - Connection pool: PgBouncer in DR region reconnects clients to new primary
   - Total: ~80 seconds — just within 5-minute RTO

4. Design PITR recovery for data errors (not hardware failure):
   - Scenario: developer deletes wrong rows at 10:45 AM
   - Recovery target: 10:44:59 (1 second before deletion)
   - Recovery server: spin up isolated instance (not the live DR standby)
   - Restore: backup from 9 AM + WAL from 9 AM → 10:44:59
   - Export corrupted table from recovery server → import to production

5. Design the 7-year WAL retention:
   - First 30 days: S3 Standard (fast access, higher cost)
   - Days 31–365: S3 Infrequent Access (slower, cheaper)
   - Years 2–7: S3 Glacier Deep Archive (very slow, very cheap)
   - Retrieval test: annually, retrieve a 3-year-old WAL segment and verify it decrypts

6. Write the DR architecture document:
   - Architecture diagram
   - Component roles and responsibilities
   - Recovery procedures (automated and manual paths)
   - Cost estimate
   - Limitations and known risks

TASK TITLE: Design a Capacity Planning and FinOps Model for Database Infrastructure
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
Cloud costs are elastic — they grow with usage and shrink with optimisation.
A FinOps approach applies financial discipline to database infrastructure:
right-size instances, eliminate waste, forecast accurately, and justify spend.

SCENARIO
Your PostgreSQL fleet on AWS costs £250,000/year. The CFO asks: "Are we
using what we pay for? Where is there waste? What do we need to buy next year?"

YOUR TASK (Capacity planning + FinOps model)
1. Build a utilisation model:
   - For each instance: average CPU %, RAM %, disk % over 90 days
   - Classify: over-provisioned (< 30% utilisation), right-sized (30-70%), under-provisioned (> 70%)
   - Finding: 40% of instances are over-provisioned (running on larger instances than needed)

2. Calculate the optimisation opportunity:
   - 24 over-provisioned instances, average instance size: db.r5.2xlarge (£400/month)
   - Right-sized target: db.r5.xlarge (£200/month)
   - Annual saving: 24 × £200 × 12 = £57,600/year

3. Identify waste categories:
   - Idle dev databases running 24/7 (should stop outside business hours → 65% saving)
   - Unused read replicas (provisioned for peak that passed)
   - Over-retained backups (keeping 60 days when policy says 30)

4. Build the forecast model:
   - Current growth rate: 15% per year (more databases, larger sizes)
   - Forecast: next year's cost = £250k × 1.15 = £287k
   - With optimisations: £250k - £57k savings = £193k × 1.15 growth = £222k
   - Presenting: "We can grow 15% while spending 11% less than today"

5. Design the rightsizing programme:
   - Monthly: review utilisation report
   - Downsize over-provisioned instances (during maintenance windows)
   - Upsize under-provisioned instances (proactively, before performance degrades)

6. Build the FinOps dashboard:
   - Current month cost per team
   - Cost trend (last 12 months)
   - Rightsizing opportunities
   - Forecast vs budget

SAFETY
Risk: Medium — rightsizing must be tested first. A database that appears
idle may have weekly batch peaks. Check 90-day history, not just average.

Area  : Performance and Monitoring
Skill : FinOps and capacity planning — utilisation analysis, cost optimisation, forecasting

TASK TITLE: Design a Database Change Management Framework at Scale
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

WHY THIS MATTERS
At L3 you implemented change management for a small team. At L4 you design
a framework for an organisation making 50+ database changes per week across
80+ databases. The process must be fast enough to support agile development
while safe enough to prevent incidents.

SCENARIO
Engineering teams make 50 database changes per week. Most are minor (adding
a column). Some are high risk (changing a column type on a 500GB table).
The current process: none. DBAs review nothing. Incidents happen every 2 weeks.

YOUR TASK (Change management framework at scale)
1. Define change risk tiers:
   - LOW RISK: additive, backward-compatible (CREATE TABLE, ADD COLUMN nullable)
     → No DBA review required, auto-approved by CI/CD
   - MEDIUM RISK: potentially disruptive (ADD INDEX on large table, ADD NOT NULL)
     → DBA async review within 4 hours, asynchronous approval
   - HIGH RISK: potentially breaking (ALTER COLUMN TYPE, DROP TABLE/COLUMN)
     → DBA synchronous review, scheduled maintenance window required
   - CRITICAL RISK: major migration (full schema refactor, version upgrade)
     → Architecture review board, phased rollout required

2. Design the automated risk classification:
   - Parse migration files in CI/CD pipeline
   - Classify based on SQL content (DROP = HIGH, ALTER TYPE = HIGH, ADD COLUMN = LOW)
   - Block high-risk changes from merging without DBA approval

3. Design the DBA review workflow:
   - DBA receives async review request with: migration SQL, table size, affected environments
   - DBA has 4 hours to respond (approve, reject, request changes)
   - If no response: escalate to next DBA in rotation
   - All reviews logged (who approved, when, what they checked)

4. Design the emergency change process:
   - Production is broken, needs immediate fix (schema change required NOW)
   - Emergency approval: 2 senior DBAs must approve within 30 minutes
   - Executed directly (bypasses normal pipeline)
   - Post-incident: root cause documented, normal change submission retroactively

5. Design metrics for the framework:
   - Time-to-approval by tier (target: LOW auto, MEDIUM < 4 hours, HIGH < 24 hours)
   - Change incident rate (changes that caused incidents / total changes)
   - Rollback rate (changes that required rollback)

6. Write the change management policy:
   - Scope (all production database changes)
   - Tier definitions and examples
   - Approval requirements per tier
   - Emergency process
   - Metrics and review cadence

SAFETY
Risk: Medium — overly strict process slows development. Overly loose process
causes incidents. Calibrate thresholds based on actual incident history.

Area  : Maintenance and Compliance
Skill : Change management framework design — risk tiers, automation, approval workflows
